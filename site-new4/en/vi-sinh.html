The Microbe Machine

A fermentation tank is a contest among three crews of tiny workers: yeasts, lactic acid bacteria and acetic acid bacteria. Choose the fermentation method, temperature and cleanliness, then drag the time to see which crew wins, how the pH falls and when to stop.

Three crews

Each crew eats one thing, needs one thing and leaves one thing behind.

Yeasts

Examples: Pichia, Candida, Saccharomyces…

Eats
Sugar in the mucilage and fruit pulp
Needs
Oxygen not required; grows faster with oxygen
Produces
Alcohol, CO₂, fruity aromatic esters, some pectin-cutting enzymes
Good when
Moderately active, giving clean fruit aromas
Harmful when
Left too long, the extra alcohol becomes feedstock for vinegar
Lactic acid bacteria

Examples: Leuconostoc, Lactiplantibacillus, Weissella…

Eats
Sugar
Needs
Little oxygen; copes with submerged and sealed tanks
Produces
Mainly lactic acid; heterofermentative types also make acetic acid, CO₂ and alcohol
Good when
Lowers pH steadily, with gentle acidity and a rounded mouthfeel
Harmful when
Tank run too long, harsh sourness
Acetic acid bacteria

Examples: Acetobacter, Gluconobacter

Eats
Alcohol made by yeasts
Needs
Oxygen, required
Produces
Acetic acid, a vinegar smell
Good when
Very little, adding a touch of wine-like brightness
Harmful when
Open tanks, lots of stirring, thick damp drying layers: vinegar smell, harsh sourness

Mucilage: food for microbes

Mucilage is the clear, slippery layer clinging to the parchment, between the fruit pulp and the bean. It is mostly water. The dry part consists of free sugars (glucose, fructose, sucrose), pectin and other fibers, plus a little organic acid, amino acids and minerals.

Pectin is what makes it slimy. When researchers separated the alcohol-insoluble part of the mucilage, they found about 30% pectic substances, 8% cellulose and 18% other neutral polysaccharides. Fermentation breaks down the pectin so the mucilage comes off when washed; the sugar feeds the microbes.

Natural keeps the whole cherry, so microbes have both the pulp and the mucilage to eat. Honey keeps part of the mucilage during drying. Washed ferments and then washes the mucilage off. See Cherry Anatomy.

Where the microbes come from

Microbes don't appear out of nowhere in the tank. They come in with the cherries and with everything that touches them. Every farm and every mill has its own microbial community, which is why the same coffee variety smells different from place to place.

Cherry skin

Microbes already live on the skin and stem. Bruised and overripe cherries carry more.

Soil

Fallen cherries, bags set on the ground, wheels and feet carry soil into the mill.

Water

Pulping water, soaking water, washing water. Dirty water poured into a tank adds contaminant microbes.

Air

Dust and spores drifting through the mill, especially near the composting husk pile.

Equipment

Tanks, pulpers, bags and baskets that aren't properly washed keep the microbes of the previous batch.

People

Hands, clothes and boots of pickers and tank workers.

MeDili reads it again

Six things about microbes in the tank.

Fermentation starts at picking

Microbes are already on the cherry skins, in the soil, water and air, and on the pickers' hands. Cherries left in bags overnight have already started fermenting, even before reaching the tank. That's why same-day processing matters as much as the tank recipe.

Longer doesn't mean better

Once the sugar runs out, the tank adds no more sweetness, only acid. An open tank gives acetic acid bacteria time to turn alcohol into vinegar; a dirty tank lets contaminant microbes produce butyric and propionic acids, signs of overfermentation.

Low pH doesn't mean tasty

pH tells you how sour the tank is, not which acid makes it sour. At the same pH 4.0, a tank rich in lactic acid tastes rounded, while one rich in acetic acid smells of vinegar. Measuring pH must go hand in hand with smelling and tasting.

Acetic acid isn't always from acetic acid bacteria

Heterofermentative lactic acid bacteria make lactic acid, acetic acid, CO₂ and alcohol, so even a sealed tank has a little acetic acid. Large amounts usually come from acetic acid bacteria when oxygen is present.

Lactic acid bacteria don't make coffee sweeter

They eat sugar and make acid. Their contribution is a pleasant acidity and a rounded mouthfeel; the sweetness in the cup comes from the sugar left in the bean and from roasting.

Oxygen decides who wins

Acetic acid bacteria need oxygen to turn alcohol into vinegar. Submerged or sealed tanks hold them back; open lids, lots of stirring and thick, damp drying layers make them stronger.

Tank log sheet

Copy the sheet, print it and stick it next to the tank. Measure pH, Brix and temperature together and note the smell; stop by pH and smell, and use the clock only to plan.
