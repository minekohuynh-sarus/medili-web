The Aroma Precursor Machine

Green coffee beans have almost no aroma. The aroma in the cup is built from what the bean has already stored: sugars, amino acids, oils, organic acids, chlorogenic acids, caffeine and trigonelline. Oil in the cup by brewing method

Coffee oil carries two diterpenes, cafestol and kahweol. Paper filters hold back almost all of them; metal filters and long boiling let a lot through.

Brewing methodOil, diterpenes in the cup
Paper filterAlmost none
PhinSome passes through the metal filter; no specific measurements yet
French pressLots of oil and fines, full body
EspressoAbout 1 mg of cafestol and 1 mg of kahweol per cup
Nordic boiled coffeeAbout 7 mg of cafestol per cup

How much caffeine reaches the cup depends on the dose and brewing method. Work it out from your habits in Coffee and the Body, and see which compounds come out first in the Extraction Machine.

Acidity: pH vs. titratable acidity

pH measures the concentration of free H⁺ ions at the moment of measuring. Titratable acidity measures the total amount of acid, as the amount of base needed to bring the liquid to a set pH, and is closer to how sour it tastes on the tongue.

Hot-brewed coffee and cold brew have similar pH, about 4.85–5.13, but the hot cup has higher titratable acidity, so it tastes more sour.

The acids in the cup are a whole system: citric and malic acids already in the bean decline during roasting; acetic and formic acids form during roasting and then evaporate; quinic acid rises with roast degree as chlorogenic acids break down. Brewing water high in alkalinity neutralizes some of them and flattens the acidity.

Seven stages that protect aroma precursors

Precursors are stored up on the farm, transformed in fermentation, preserved through drying and storage, cleared of defects in sorting, and only then unlocked in the roaster. If one stage fails, the roaster can't save it.

  1. 01 · StoreRipe cherries

    Beans store enough sugar, protein and oil when the cherries are properly ripe. Pick them green and the beans lack precursors from the start.

  2. 02 · TransformFermentation

    Microbes eat the mucilage and create acids and esters; overfermentation pulls vinegar and onion smells into the beans.

  3. 03 · PreserveSun-drying, machine drying

    Bring bean moisture down to 10–12% evenly. Thick layers and trapped moisture invite mold; drying too hot strips the aroma.

  4. 04 · PreserveStorage

    A dry, cool, ventilated store with bags raised off the floor. Beans kept too long, damp or hot lose aroma and pick up jute-bag and stale smells.

  5. 05 · Remove defectsSorting, color sorting

    Remove stones, metal, black beans, insect-damaged beans and fermented beans. One defective bean is enough to ruin the smell of a whole roast batch.

  6. 06 · Even outSize and density grading

    Beans of the same size and density roast evenly; small beans mixed with large ones end up burnt and underdone at the same time.

  7. 07 · UnlockRoasting

    The roaster doesn't create aroma from nothing; it turns existing precursors into hundreds of volatile aroma compounds.

Six things about beans and aroma.

More bitter doesn't mean more caffeine

Caffeine is bitter but makes up only 1–2.5% of the bean. The cup's bitterness also comes from chlorogenic acid breakdown products and Maillard reaction products, which increase with darker roasts. Robusta is more bitter because it has more of both caffeine and chlorogenic acids than Arabica.

Dark roasting doesn't increase caffeine

Caffeine is heat-stable and is barely lost in roasting. On a dry-matter basis the figure creeps up slightly because the beans lose mass: Arabica from 1.2 to 1.3%, Robusta from 2.2 to 2.4% at a medium roast. Measured by the scoop, dark-roasted beans are lighter and more porous, so each scoop has less caffeine.

More oil doesn't mean more body

A cup's body comes from oil, suspended fines, melanoidins and dissolved polysaccharides. A paper filter holds back almost all the oil, but filter coffee still has body if it is extracted enough.

Robusta has about 10% oil, Arabica about 16%

According to commonly used composition tables, green Arabica contains about 16% lipids and Robusta about 10%, on a dry-matter basis. Figures vary between sources, but Arabica always has more oil.

The embryo is tiny; the bean is almost all endosperm

The embryo is the seedling at one end of the bean, just a few millimeters long. Nearly all of the bean's mass is endosperm, where the plant stores sugar, protein, oil and the aroma precursors.

About 900 volatiles, but fewer than 20 define the aroma

About 900 volatile compounds have been identified in roasted coffee, but fewer than 20 play a clear role in its aroma, such as 2-furfurylthiol (roasty), furaneol (caramel) and 4-vinylguaiacol (clove).
